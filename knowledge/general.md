Fundamentals of Building Maintenance


Introduction
Purpose of Building Maintenance
The purpose of building maintenance is not just to "maintain" the existing condition of facilities. The purpose is to improve the condition of facilities for the benefit and especially the health and safety of building occupants.
Key Benefits
Building maintenance helps ensure:

Facilities and all related components (assets) achieve their Maximum Lifecycle Expectancy (MLE)
Prevention of further deterioration of components
Development of 1, 3, 5, and 10-year capital improvement and replacement plans

Long-term Benefits for Building Owners

Reduced maintenance costs
Reduced expenditures
Overall cost savings

Maintenance Philosophy
Proper building maintenance is always PROACTIVE, not reactive or corrective.
Maintenance Types

Proactive Maintenance: Actions taken BEFORE a failure has occurred
Reactive/Corrective/Breakdown Maintenance: Repairing or replacing an asset AFTER it has broken down


Definitions
Assets
Physical assets for facility management include all related building structures, components, equipment, and parts. Examples include:

Building structures
Roofs
Doors and windows
Parking lots
Lights
Boilers and furnaces
Motors and pumps
Hot water tanks

Preventative Maintenance (PM)
Regularly scheduled inspections, tests, servicing, replacements, repairs, and other tasks intended to reduce the impact and frequency of equipment failures.
Predictive Maintenance
"Advanced" preventative maintenance utilizing specialized equipment and methods to predict future equipment breakdowns, including:

Thermal imaging
Vibration detectors
Oil analysis
Boiler water sampling

Note: Predictive maintenance should not be considered separate from preventative maintenance, as many predictive tests assist in PM activities.
Corrective Maintenance (Reactive or Breakdown Maintenance)
Maintenance that occurs after an asset has failed. Designed to identify and correct the failed component by repair or replacement. Often a result of "run to fail" directives.

Safety

Building maintenance requires access to areas and equipment where mandatory safety training, attention, Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), and safety precautions are required.
CRITICAL: Never compromise personal or employee safety to observe or complete building maintenance tasks.

Elevated Work Safety
Ladder Safety

4-to-1 rule (4 feet vertical for every 1 foot horizontal)

Roof Work

Electrical Safety
When working around electrical devices or components:

Ensure all electrical power is turned off
Implement proper lockout/tagout procedures
Verify power is off before beginning work

Mechanical and HVAC Equipment Safety
Special attention required due to multiple hazards:
Hazards Include:

Heat/Flame: Inside furnaces, boilers, hot water tanks, vents
Electrical: Power to units, components, breaker panels
Moving Parts: Motors, fans, pulleys, belts
High Pressure: Compressors, boilers
Chemicals: Glycol, water treatment products
Hot Water: Boiler systems, hot water tanks, relief valves

Professional Services
Major, specialized building maintenance requires services of trained professional service providers.
Regulatory Compliance
All First Nations, Provincial, and Federal safety procedures and guidelines must be followed.

Proper Building Preventative Maintenance
Foundation of PM Programs
A proper preventative maintenance program begins with:

Complete inspection of all buildings, facilities, equipment, and components
Documentation of all information and pictures in an Asset Condition Report

Program Requirements
A preventive maintenance program should be:

Well-defined
Periodically reviewed
Adjusted as necessary
Written in sufficient detail for each piece of equipment

Importance of Preventive Maintenance
Purpose
The purpose is to maintain equipment in optimum working condition and prevent unplanned downtime due to breakdowns.
Cost-Benefit

Replacing components prior to failure costs far less than consequences of failure during service
Preventing problems is almost always less expensive than addressing actual failures
Despite potential budget pressures, cutting maintenance budgets is false economy

Asset Management
Inventory and Tracking

All assets must be properly inventoried, logged, and tagged
Asset numbers should be created for key assets (especially HVAC)
Maintain comprehensive asset database

Building Envelope
Definition
The building envelope includes all components that separate indoors from outdoors:

Exterior walls
Foundations
Roofs
Windows
Doors

Performance Factors
Building envelope performance is impacted by:

Heating, cooling, and ventilating equipment
Plumbing systems
Electrical systems
Occupant activities

Functions of Building Envelope
The building envelope must keep out:

Temperature extremes
Moisture (as vapor or liquid)
Dust
Wind

Durability Requirements
To maintain durability, the building envelope should not:

Permit weather elements to be trapped inside walls
Allow moisture accumulation that causes deterioration

Early detection and remediation: Problems caught early can usually be remedied relatively inexpensively. Costs increase exponentially with time.
Signs of Building Envelope Problems
A problem likely exists if there is:

No regular inspection and preventative maintenance program
Mold or fungi formation
Wood rot
Water flowing down building sides instead of through eaves and drainpipes
Wind blowing through walls
Peeling paint
Cracked or missing sealants (caulking)
Water stains on inside walls, ceilings, or foundations
Gaps allowing weather penetration
Pools of water on decks
Windows wet on the inside


Building Envelope: Roofs
Safety First
BEFORE attempting roof surface inspections, ensure all safety precautions for roof safety and ladder use are followed.
Visual Inspection Components
1. Debris
Problem:

Any debris on roof surface causes:

Pooling or ponding
Potential membrane penetration
Premature wear
Moss accumulation



Maintenance:

Clear debris regularly, especially:

After fall season
Before snow accumulation



Prevention:

Tall trees or shrubs near buildings are constant debris sources
Consider vegetation management

2. Pooling and Ponding
Causes:

Blocked drains and downspouts (most common)
Negative slope (away from drains/scuppers)
Lack of proper/sufficient drainage
Roof deficiencies

Consequences:

Premature roof deterioration
Vegetation growth
Structural concerns

Action Required:

Clear blocked drains immediately
Assess for structural/design issues if pooling persists

3. Buckling, Blistering, or Cracking Membrane
Characteristics:

Common in Built-Up Roof (BUR) systems
Indicates separation of roof felts
Moisture entrapment
Membrane seam failure

Action:

Document location and extent
Plan for repair or replacement
Monitor for progression

4. Dark Areas
Indication:

Extensive standing water
Mold and algae growth
Long-term moisture exposure

Action:

Assess drainage
Clean affected areas
Address underlying moisture source

5. Exterior Roof Components
Components to Inspect:

Flashing
Scuppers
Drains
Downspouts

Common Issues:

Faulty or missing components
Flashing pulling away from walls
Deteriorated sealants

Maintenance:

Reseal around flashings and drains after deficiencies noted
Check tight seams joining flashing sections
Ensure all components are secure

6. Penetrations (HVAC/Mechanical Components)
Vulnerability:

Any roof membrane penetration is potential water infiltration source

Common Problems:

Mastic/sealant deterioration
Hard, brittle, and cracked sealant
Gaps around:

Vents
Chimneys
Skylights
HVAC piping, tubing, conduits



Maintenance:

Reseal when deficiencies noted
Use appropriate sealant for roof type

7. Foot Traffic
Concerns:

Most membranes not designed for heavy, continual foot traffic
Premature erosion of granulation
Indentations
Seam damage

Best Practices:

Limit access to necessary personnel
Use designated walkways when available
Consider raised walkways for frequently accessed areas

8. Forces of Nature
Impact Factors:

Excess snow
Wind
Rain
Hail

Action Required:

Conduct visual/professional inspection after major storms
Document damage
Address issues promptly

Deterioration:

Flat roofs lose protective covering from sun, rain, and wind exposure over time
This natural wear is how leaks start

Masonry Walls (Brick, Concrete Block)
Primary Concerns:

Cracking
Water intrusion

Water Effects on Masonry:

Erodes mortar (binding cement at joints)
Causes mortar disintegration over time
Creates cracks (more water entry points)
Creates efflorescence (whitish film from dried mineral salts)

Efflorescence Process:

Water in masonry picks up minerals
Water meets outside air and evaporates
Mineral salts remain as residue

Tuck-Pointing:
When mortar falls out of joints:

Fresh mortar must be applied
Use small triangular-shaped trowel
Fill all open joints

Wainscot Issues:
Problems with external masonry wall covering:

Paint peeling or flaking (due to moisture)
Cracking (due to settlement)
Cracks must be filled to prevent water infiltration and further deterioration

Joint Maintenance:
Critical area: Joint between masonry and other materials (wood/metal trim on doors and windows)
Why joints fail:

Dissimilar materials
Different rates of expansion and contraction
Movement opens joints even if caulked
Allows water entry

Solution:

Use high-grade exterior caulk
Regular inspection and re-caulking

Wood Siding Walls
Types:

Planks or boards (vertical or horizontal installation)
Wood shingles
Panels (exterior grade plywood or composite materials like hardboard or wafer-board)

Vulnerability:
All wood siding products are vulnerable to water damage.
Two Simple Rules:

Protective Coating: Wood must be kept coated with protective film:

Paint, OR
Stain with wood preservative


Joint Sealing: All joints and openings must be caulked to prevent water intrusion

Signs of Coating Failure:

Coating starts to wear
Loses ability to shed water
Underlying wood becomes susceptible to:

Water intrusion
Warping
Rotting



Inspection Focus:

Peeling or flaking paint
Stains worn so thin that wood grain is exposed and raised
Open joints (however small)
Water penetration points

Note: Open joints pose problems even if paint/stain coating is in prime condition.
Importance of Early Action:

Take action BEFORE wood rot sets in
Once rot occurs, must tear off rotted section and install new materials (always more expensive)

Repainting/Re-staining Schedule:
Wood Plank Siding or Shingles:

Maximum interval: 7-10 years
Better practice: 5-7 year cycle

Other Wood Materials:

Much shorter intervals required
Exterior Plywood:

Check annually
May need more frequent treatment


Particle Board:

Touch-up every year needed
Prevents warping and disintegration



Critical Areas:
Exposed edges of any wood siding material (plank, shingle, or panel):

Most likely points for water penetration
Must be well-sealed with paint and caulk

Long-term Consequences
Unnoticed Deficiencies:

May go unnoticed for years
Eventually cause extensive damage to:

Exterior
Interior


Become health and safety hazard

Historical Example: British Columbia "Leaky Condo Crisis"

Occurred in 1980s and 1990s
Over 900 buildings severely impacted
By 2003: ~65,000 leaky condos identified across province
Defined as "catastrophic failure" of building envelopes
Water infiltration led to:

Rot
Rust
Decay

Economic impact: $3-5 billion estimated cost
Causes: Combination of poor design and shoddy construction during building boom
Human impact: Many homeowners went bankrupt facing thousands in repairs

Interior walls

Symptoms of Foundation Failure
Interior Symptoms:

Noticeable vertical cracks in foundations
Water penetration and seepage on walls with white staining (efflorescence)
Water pooling on basement floors
Noticeable water staining on basement floors
Uneven basement or other floors
Noticeable gaps or spaces between interior/exterior walls and adjacent windows/doors becoming wider

Exterior Symptoms:

Outside surfaces around walls have negative slope toward building
Noticeable cracks in foundation material (concrete, bricks, etc.)
Noticeable cracks on building cladding, especially around doors and windows

Repair Options
Minor Cracks:
Epoxy Injection Method:

Epoxy mix injected into cracks
If done properly:

Epoxy seeps through cracks
Penetrates to exterior
Provides "leak-proof" repair



Note: Tar coating on inside of wall:

Does NOT penetrate to exterior
Does NOT entirely solve water infiltration problem

Major Damage:
Requires extensive work:

Exterior trenching
Apply membrane coat:

Tar
Polyurethane product
Dimpled elastomeric membrane
Plastic membrane


Install/reinstall weeping tile
Proper drainage system
Backfill with appropriate materials

Janitorial Services: Cleaning vs. Disinfecting vs. Sanitizing
Cleaning
Definition:

Removes germs, dirt, and impurities from surfaces or objects

How It Works:

Uses soap (or detergent) and water to physically remove germs from surfaces

Results:

Does not necessarily kill germs
By removing them, lowers their numbers
Lowers risk of spreading infection

Disinfecting
Definition:

Kills germs on surfaces or objects

How It Works:

Uses chemicals to kill germs on surfaces or objects

Results:

Does not necessarily clean dirty surfaces
Does not necessarily remove germs
By killing germs on surface after cleaning, further lowers risk of spreading infection

Sanitizing
Definition:

Lowers number of germs on surfaces or objects to safe level
As judged by public health standards or requirements

How It Works:

Works by either cleaning or disinfecting surfaces or objects

Results:

Lowers risk of spreading infection
